## The Day the Checklist Was Born

On 30 October 1935, the United States Army Air Corps gathered at Wright Field in Dayton, Ohio, to watch a flying competition that was widely considered a formality.

Boeing's entrant, the Model 299, was the aircraft that would later become the B-17 Flying Fortress. It was faster than its rivals, carried more bombs, and flew farther. Boeing had mortgaged itself to build it. Everyone expected it to win.

The aircraft taxied, accelerated, lifted off, climbed to roughly three hundred feet, stalled, and fell back to earth in front of the assembled brass. Two of the five men aboard were killed, including Major Ployer Peter Hill, chief of the field's Flying Branch and one of the most accomplished test pilots in the country.

The investigation found no mechanical fault. Hill had simply forgotten to release the gust locks — a mechanism that immobilises the tail control surfaces so wind cannot damage them while the aircraft is parked.

A superb pilot, flying a superb aircraft, was killed by a step he knew perfectly well and did not perform.

Newspapers concluded the Model 299 was too much airplane for one man to fly. The investigating team reached a more precise and far more useful verdict: it was **not too much airplane for one man to fly — it was too complex for any one man's memory.**

That distinction changed aviation. Boeing's engineers and test pilots did not respond by demanding better pilots or more training hours. They wrote a list. Critical actions for taxi, takeoff, and landing, printed on a card, to be read and confirmed aloud before every flight.

The aircraft that failed its own demonstration flight went on to become one of the most successful bombers ever built.

## Why the Checklist Spread

Seventy-four years later, a surgeon named Atul Gawande led a team that asked whether the same idea could work in operating theatres. The World Health Organization developed a nineteen-item surgical safety checklist and tested it across eight hospitals on four continents — Tanzania, the Philippines, India, Jordan, the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and New Zealand — covering 7,688 patients before and after adoption.

The results, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2009, were difficult to argue with. Major complications fell from 11% to 7%. Inpatient deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8% — a reduction of more than forty percent.

Nineteen items. No new drugs, no new equipment, no new surgical technique. The surgeons already knew everything on the list.

That is the part worth sitting with.

Gawande draws a distinction that matters enormously to investors. Some failures are **errors of ignorance** — we fail because the knowledge does not yet exist. Others are **errors of ineptitude** — the knowledge exists, and we simply fail to apply it. Ignorance we forgive. Ineptitude we find intolerable, and yet it is overwhelmingly the more common cause of disaster in any mature, complex field.

Checklists do nothing for ignorance. Against ineptitude they are remarkably effective.

## Investing Is a Discipline of Ineptitude

Ask an experienced investor what destroyed their worst position and you will rarely hear about a concept they had never encountered.

You will hear that they knew the balance sheet was stretched. That they knew the valuation required heroic assumptions. That they knew the management team had a history of self-dealing. That they knew the business sat outside their competence. That they knew the position had grown too large.

They knew. Under enthusiasm, time pressure, social proof, or the particular vertigo of watching a price run away from them, they did not check.

This is precisely the Wright Field failure mode. Not insufficient skill — insufficient process.

Charlie Munger was explicit about the remedy:

> "I'm a great believer in solving hard problems by using a checklist. You need to get all the likely and unlikely answers before you, otherwise it's easy to miss something important."

Mohnish Pabrai took the idea further than most, building an investment checklist assembled not from theory but from forensic study of actual mistakes — his own and those of investors he admired. Each documented error became a permanent question.

That is the correct way to build one. A checklist is not a summary of what you believe. It is a record of how you have been hurt.

## Before the Checklist: The Preflight Gate

A pilot does not begin the takeoff checklist until the aircraft is the right aircraft for the mission. Investors need the same gate, and it comes before any of the four M's.

**Can I explain this business, in writing, in five sentences, to someone who has never heard of it?**

If not, stop. Nothing further on the list can rescue you.

This is Munger's first filter, and it is the one most commonly skipped. Buffett and Munger's stated process was always four questions in sequence: a business we can **understand**, with a durable competitive **advantage**, run by able and trustworthy **management**, available at a sensible **price**.

Notice that the popular Four M's framework — Moat, Management, Margin, Momentum — contains three of those four and drops the first. In Phil Town's original formulation the four M's begin with **Meaning**, which is the same gate under a different name: does this business mean something to you, do you understand how it earns money, would you be comfortable owning all of it?

Keep the gate. Then proceed.

## The Four M's

### M1 — Moat

The question is not whether the business is good today. It is whether it will still be good after a well-funded competitor spends five years trying to take it apart.

- Can I name the source of the moat specifically — switching costs, network effects, scale economics, regulatory position, brand, or low-cost production? *"Good management"* and *"great product"* are not moats.
- Has return on invested capital stayed meaningfully above the cost of capital for a full decade, including a recession?
- Could this business raise prices by 10% and retain its customers? If yes, why has it not?
- Is the moat **widening or narrowing**? Check five years of market share and gross margin, not one.
- If a competitor with unlimited capital attacked tomorrow, what specifically stops them?

Ackman's criteria converge here: he looks for a **dominant market position**, **large barriers to entry**, and **high returns on capital**.

### M2 — Management

Management is not assessed through interviews or impressions. It is assessed through history.

- What did they actually do with a decade of free cash flow? Reinvest, acquire, buy back stock, pay dividends — and at what prices?
- Were buybacks executed when the stock was cheap, or when it was expensive and the optics were convenient?
- How are they paid? Which metrics trigger the bonus, and can those metrics be gamed at the expense of long-term value?
- Do they own meaningful stock they purchased, as distinct from stock they were granted?
- Read the last five shareholder letters. Do they name their mistakes? Compare each year's promises against the following year's results.
- Any related-party transactions, auditor changes, restatements, or unexplained executive departures?

Ackman's formulation is short: **excellent management and good governance.** The checklist is how you verify it rather than assume it.

### M3 — Margin of Safety

The discipline here is sequence. Value the business first, then look at the price. Reversing that order lets the price anchor the valuation, and you will unconsciously justify whatever the market is asking.

- Have I written down an intrinsic value estimate, with assumptions, **before** checking the quote?
- What discount to that estimate am I being offered, and is it wide enough to absorb my own error?
- Run it backwards: what growth and margin does today's price already assume? Is that assumption aggressive or conservative?
- In a genuine downside case — recession, key customer lost, margin compression — what do I lose permanently, as opposed to temporarily?
- Could the business survive two years of falling revenue **without raising external capital**?
- Is the position sized so that being wrong is survivable?

Three of Ackman's commandments live here: the business should be **simple and predictable**, **free-cash-flow generative**, and carry a **strong balance sheet that does not depend on access to capital to survive**. His reasoning on predictability is the cleanest statement of why this matters:

> "If you believe that the value of anything financial is the present value of the cash you can take out of it over its life, you need to know how much that will be. If we can't predict the cash flows, we don't know what it's worth."

Unpredictable businesses are not merely riskier. They are *unvaluable* — and a margin of safety cannot be measured against a number you were never able to calculate.

### M4 — Momentum

This is the item that requires the most care, because there are two entirely different things called momentum and Munger would have endorsed only one of them.

**Price momentum** — buying because the chart is rising — is closer to the opposite of everything above. It substitutes the crowd's opinion for your own analysis, and it systematically pushes you toward businesses at their most expensive and away from them at their cheapest.

**Business momentum** is the direction of the underlying economics, and it is a legitimate and important question:

- Over three to five years, which way are revenue, free cash flow per share, and return on invested capital actually moving?
- Is there a **reinvestment runway** — can the company deploy tomorrow's capital at today's high rates of return? A compounding machine needs somewhere to put the money.
- Are unit economics improving or quietly deteriorating beneath a growing top line?
- Is growth *funding itself*, or consuming capital and issuing shares to continue?
- Is the narrative running ahead of the numbers? Which specific figures would confirm or destroy the story in the next four quarters?

Ackman's remaining commandment belongs here: **limited exposure to extrinsic risks the company cannot control.** A business whose trajectory depends on commodity prices, interest rates, a single customer, or a regulator's mood does not have durable momentum. It has weather.

## The Non-Normal Checklist

Aviation carries two sets of checklists. Normal procedures for routine flight, and non-normal procedures for when something has gone wrong — engine fire, hydraulic failure, loss of pressurisation. The second set exists because the moment you most need clear thinking is the moment you are least capable of it.

Investors need the second set far more than they admit, and they almost never write it.

Munger's favoured tool was inversion: rather than asking how to succeed, ask what guarantees failure, then refuse to do it. An abort list should be written calmly, in advance, and consulted when calm is unavailable:

- The thesis has changed, but I have changed it *after* the fact to accommodate the price.
- I am buying because it has already risen, or averaging down purely because it has fallen.
- I cannot articulate what would make me sell.
- The reason I originally bought has been factually disproven, and I am holding for a different reason.
- Accounting has become harder to follow, not easier, as I have learned more.
- Refinancing is required in a market that may not be open.
- Position size has drifted to where a permanent loss would impair the whole portfolio.
- I am outside the preflight gate and proceeding anyway.

There is one more borrowing worth making. Aviation has a **sterile cockpit rule**: below ten thousand feet, no conversation unrelated to flying the aircraft. During the phases of highest consequence, noise is prohibited by procedure rather than by willpower.

Market panics and manias are the investor's ten thousand feet. Decide in advance whose commentary you will not read.

## How to Actually Fly It

A checklist that is not used is not a checklist. It is a document. Four points determine whether yours survives contact with a real decision:

1. **Write it before you buy, and date it.** A checklist completed afterward is a rationalisation with formatting.
2. **Keep it to one page.** Gawande found that lists which grow past roughly the length of a single screen get skipped precisely when they matter most. Nineteen items reduced surgical mortality by forty percent. You do not need ninety.
3. **Use challenge and response.** In a cockpit, one pilot reads the item and the other confirms it aloud. Reading silently to yourself permits the eye to slide past an uncomfortable line. Say the answer out loud, or write it.
4. **Feed it from your own post-mortems.** Keep a written record of every closed position — thesis, outcome, and what you now know you missed. When a mistake repeats, it becomes a permanent line on the list. This is the Pabrai method, and it is what separates a living checklist from a borrowed one.

And the most important structural rule: **a checklist is a gate, not a score.** You do not average the items and proceed on a passing grade. A single unresolved item on the moat, the balance sheet, or the preflight gate is a reason to stop — exactly as one unreleased gust lock was sufficient to bring down an aircraft that was superior in every other respect.

## The Kuberin Perspective

At Kuberin, we believe the quality of long-term returns is determined far less by brilliance than by the reliability of process under pressure.

Markets are complex, fast, and emotionally loud — the conditions in which human memory is least trustworthy and most confident. The four filters have been public for decades. Ackman's eight commandments are literally engraved on stone tablets on every desk at his firm. None of this is secret knowledge.

The advantage was never in knowing the list. It is in running the list, every time, including the times when it is inconvenient and especially the times when you are certain you do not need to.

> Great pilots are not the ones who never forget. They are the ones who never rely on remembering.

## Final Thoughts

Major Hill knew about gust locks. He had probably released them a thousand times. On 30 October 1935, in front of an audience, flying the most advanced aircraft in the world, he did not.

The response to that crash is the part of the story worth carrying into investing. Aviation did not conclude that it needed better pilots. It concluded that competence alone was an insufficient system, and it built a mechanism that made expertise reliable.

You will not become a great investor by writing a checklist. But you may avoid the small number of catastrophic, entirely foreseeable errors that separate a compounding record from a crater — and in a discipline where permanent loss is the one truly unrecoverable event, avoiding the crater is most of the job.

Understand the business. Demand a moat. Study the management. Insist on a margin of safety. Watch the fundamentals, not the chart. Write down what would make you abort.

Then read it aloud before every takeoff.

> **Checklists do not make pilots smarter. They make pilots survive long enough for their skill to compound.**

## Method Note

The Four M's in this article are presented as Moat, Management, Margin of Safety, and Momentum. Readers familiar with Phil Town's original framework will know it as Meaning, Moat, Management, and Margin of Safety; that first element is preserved here as the preflight gate, since understanding a business is a precondition rather than a criterion.

Momentum is deliberately restricted to **business** momentum. Neither Munger nor Ackman advocates buying on price strength, and nothing in this article should be read as endorsing that practice.

The checklist items are illustrative prompts, not a complete or validated diligence framework, and any quantitative anchors are starting points to be adjusted for industry, cycle, and mandate. A checklist is only useful when it is built from the holder's own documented mistakes.
